MealVitals

Market-to-table meal planning for Singaporeans managing chronic conditions, and the caregivers who cook for them.

Problem

About one in three Singapore residents has hyperlipidaemia or hypertension, and awareness campaigns have not turned into better daily food choices. The target population is the 1.1 million residents aged 18+ diagnosed with or at risk of chronic conditions, plus the caregivers who plan their meals. Three gaps stand in the way:

1Labels are hard to read68% don't know the daily salt limit, 83% the sodium limit, and 30% find the Nutrition Information Panel confusing.
2Planning takes effortEven people who know the healthier choice drop it when they have to plan every meal and shop for it.
3Elderly users struggle with appsMany diners with chronic conditions can't manage a digital tool on their own, so the burden lands on family.

Key objective

Make dietary management easy to stick to: turn a diner's health profile, nutrient limits and food preferences into a week of meals and a grocery list, automatically. MealVitals recommends meals; it does not give medical advice.

Solution

Six features, each mapped to the gap it closes. Clips and screenshots are from the running app.

How it's built

StackReact + Vite frontend, FastAPI backend, PostgreSQL via async SQLAlchemy, OR-Tools CP-SAT for planning. Containerised with Docker, deployed to Google Cloud Run through GitHub Actions.
Why cloudZero upfront CAPEX and a lower three-year total cost of ownership than two on-premises racks sized for year-3 traffic.
Business modelFreemium: planning and health tracking are free; personalised auto-fill and dynamic recommendations are premium.
Projected usersAt 2% adoption of the 1.1M target population: 4,400 monthly active users in year 1, 14,300 in year 2 and 22,000 by year 3.

Plant Disease Classification

ResNet-18 with Pyramid Squeeze Attention, trained on lab images and adapted to real field photos.

Problem

Spotting plant disease from a leaf photo means reading subtle cues: lesion patterns, texture, colour shifts and shape changes that differ by disease stage. The training data is PlantVillage, 54,305 lab images across 14 species and 38 classes; the real test is PlantDoc, 2,578 photos taken in the field. Three gaps stand in the way:

1Depth is not freeDeeper plain CNNs hit the degradation problem, where adding layers raises training error, and they can attend to background instead of the small diseased regions.
2Lab images are not field imagesPlantVillage shows one leaf on a grey background. PlantDoc has mixed lighting, angles and clutter. A model that is near perfect in the lab can collapse in the field.
3Classes are imbalancedThe largest PlantVillage class has 36× more images than the smallest, so a naive model learns the common diseases and ignores the rare ones.

Key objective

Test whether a simple ResNet-18, trained the way Castilla et al. (2022) trained their diabetic retinopathy baseline, can reach a high AUC on 38-class plant disease classification, and then measure how far discriminative fine-tuning closes the gap between lab images and field images.

Solution

Six steps in the order they were built, each mapped to the gap it closes.

Results

In the lab: 99.89% accuracy, AUC 1.000ResNet-18 replicates Castilla et al.'s strong baseline on a 38-class problem. Label smoothing and PSA do not change lab accuracy but help generalisation later.
In the field: 6.2% → 50.4% accuracyDiscriminative fine-tuning on PlantDoc lifts accuracy from 6.21% to 50.35%, macro F1 from 0.05 to 0.44, and AUC from 0.57 to 0.94.
No trade-off in the labAfter fine-tuning, PlantVillage accuracy dips only 0.36 pt to 99.48%, so the model keeps what it learned while adapting to the field.
What the AUC saysA 0.94 AUC with 50% accuracy means the model reliably ranks the right disease near the top but does not always pick it first across 38 classes. More diverse field images and style-transfer augmentation are the next steps.
